A heat pump is an air conditioner that learned to run in reverse: in summer it moves heat out of your house, and in winter a reversing valve flips the refrigerant flow so the same compressor pulls heat from outdoor air and delivers it inside. Because it moves heat rather than generating it, a heat pump routinely delivers two to three times more heat energy than the electricity it consumes, which is why so many Dunwoody, GA homeowners are switching as older equipment ages out. Modern cold-climate models with variable-speed inverter compressors keep producing useful heat far below freezing, a genuine engineering leap over the units that gave the technology its old reputation.

Great heat pump performance starts with design work, and that is where Dunwoody HVAC earns your trust. A heat pump has to be sized for two loads, summer cooling and winter heating, and the design has to find the balance point where the outdoor temperature meets the unit's capacity curve. Our technicians run the load calculation, compare single-stage, two-stage, and variable-speed inverter equipment against your climate and ductwork, and plan the backup heat properly, either electric strips staged below the balance point or a dual-fuel setup with a gas furnace that takes over in deep cold. We also check your electrical service and ducts, since heat pumps deliver lower supply temperatures than furnaces and need solid airflow to feel comfortable.

Installation follows the same discipline as every Dunwoody HVAC system: line set pressure-tested and evacuated to a micron-verified vacuum, charge weighed in and confirmed with superheat and subcooling, the outdoor unit elevated on a stand where winter defrost drainage demands it, condensate handled properly, and the thermostat configured so staging, backup lockouts, and defrost behave the way the engineering intended. Then we commission in both modes, heating and cooling, and record the readings. Why Professional Heat Pump Installation Matters

Heat pumps punish installation shortcuts more than any other residential system because they work both sides of the year. A charge that is slightly off hurts twice, in cooling efficiency all summer and heating capacity all winter. A balance point never calculated means electric backup strips quietly carrying the house at several times the operating cost while the homeowner wonders why the bills did not drop. A thermostat left on defaults can run backup heat when the compressor alone would do, or fight the defrost cycle. And an outdoor unit set flat on the ground in a real winter sits in its own refrozen defrost water until the coil is a block of ice.

We design and install to prevent every one of those problems. Loads are calculated for heating and cooling, equipment is selected for its actual capacity at your winter design temperature, backup heat is staged and locked out properly, the outdoor unit is raised for defrost drainage, and the charge is weighed and verified instead of guessed. Commissioning covers both modes, including a check of the reversing valve and a full defrost cycle. Refrigerant handling is federally regulated, and inverter equipment brings electronics that reward careful setup. Installed correctly, a heat pump gives your home the quietest, steadiest comfort available.

Maintenance Tips for Heat Pump Installation

Taking preventive measures can help you avoid costly heating and cooling problems down the road. Put these technician-approved steps to work around your property:

  • Keep an eye on winter bills; a sharp increase usually means the backup heat is running too much
  • Book twice-yearly maintenance so charge and defrost stay verified in both modes
  • Keep temperature settings steady so the backup strips are not needed to catch up
  • Give the outdoor unit open space all year, free of drifts and overgrowth
  • Light frost and an occasional defrost hiss are normal in winter, but solid ice calls for service
  • Gently hose off the outdoor coil every spring before cooling season
  • Change filters on schedule; low airflow hurts a heat pump in both seasons
